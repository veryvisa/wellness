and it plunges directly into the masseter muscle. Okay, and at the bottom of the muscle. Deep to the muscle lies the masseteric artery and masseteric nerve, sitting right on the ramus of the mandible. Safety guidelines dictate stopping at the mandible during perpendicular insertion.

Interestingly, you must avoid opening the mouth wide while the needles are retained. Yeah, that's important. Overall, it is classified as low risk. The exam points for ST6, GIACI. One finger breath, antero superior to the mandibular angle, right on the masseter bulge.

This point acts as a crossing point for the stomach and gallbladder channels.

Its location is on the face, resting in the depression between the midpoint of the inferior border of the zygomatic arch and the mandibular notch. To locate this by touch, keep your mouth closed. Place your finger about a finger breath in front of the tragus of your ear, right below the bony zygomatic arch, and you'll feel a distinct hollow. So what does this all mean for the mechanics of the jaw in this particular spot? It means the physical landscape of the point actually changes based on position.

When you open your mouth, the condyle of the jaw slides forward and completely fills that hollow. Closing the mouth restores the depression. That's wild. Yeah. The one anatomy fact that matters most here is that this hollow exists only with the mouth closed.

And deep inside that space lies the highly sensitive infratemporal fossa, containing the maxillary artery and the mandibular nerve. Because of that shifting anatomy, needling must be done perpendicularly with the mouth closed to a depth of 0.5 to 1 coon. You cannot let the patient open their mouth while the needles are retained because the shifting bone will literally bend or break the needles. Exactly.

Tracing the anatomy downward under the skin, you have the parotid gland, zygomatic branches of the facial nerve, and the transverse facial artery. And the muscle. The structural boundary is the zygomatic arch, the mandibular notch, and the posterior aspect of the temporal mandibular joint. Exam points for ST7 Chaguan, found in the hollow between the mid-zygomatic arch and mandibular notch with the mouth closed. Perpendicular insertion, 0.5 to 1 soon. You must keep the mouth closed during retention. And it is a stomach and gallbladder crossing point.

Ancient sources record it for lockjaw, facial pain, toothache, facial deviation, deafness, tinnitus, and ear discharge. This remains a traditional record, not a clinical proof. Finally, we move out of the facial region entirely and up to the head for the last point in this section, ST8 two-way, or head corner.

This point is a crossing point of the stomach and gallbladder channels, as well as the Yang way vessel. The location is on the head exactly 0.5 Tsun directly above the anterior hairline at the corner of the forehead, which is 4.5 Tsun lateral to the midline. By touch, you want to find the hairline at the corner of the forehead.

It is helpful to note that the two hairline corners are exactly nine quen apart from each other. Okay, good to know. From that corner goes straight up 0.5 quen. If you clench your teeth, you will feel the temporalis muscle contract just below this exact point. Needling here is transverse, 0.5 to 1 quen.

And according to the Jiayi Jing, traditionally there is no moxibustion applied here. Our obstacle course has completely shifted again. We are no longer dodging facial nerves or jaw mechanics. Right. The danger now shifts to the scalp.

The one anatomy fact that matters most here is that the scalp is a thin, highly vascular area. So it bleeds easily. Very easily. The critical rule is that you must keep the transverse needle perfectly positioned in the sub-oponeurotic layer, which means right under the gallia oponeurotica.

If you slip out of that precise layer, you will cause immediate and heavy bleeding, which is why you must press firmly on withdrawal. Let's trace those layers from the surface down to understand that space. Right into the skin, you have the frontal branch of the superficial temporal artery, along with branches of the auriculotemporal nerve. In the muscle and tendon layer, you find the galea aponeurotica in the upper edge of the temporalis muscle. And the deeper layers?

Deeper down are the temporal branches of the facial nerve, all resting over the frontal and parietal bones. Safety rules emphasize that because the scalp bleeds so easily, you press after withdrawal. You absolutely must keep the transverse needle safely within that sub-aponeurotic layer. Because the technique is transverse, the overall risk level is classified as low risk. Located 0.5 soon above the forehead corner hairline, 4.5 soon from the midline. Needling is transverse 0.5 to 1 soon, and it serves as a stomach, gallbladder, and yangwei crossing point. The traditional records to this point include headache, dizziness, eye pain, and tearing.
