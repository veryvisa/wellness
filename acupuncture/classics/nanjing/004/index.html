臣等謹案難經本義二卷元滑夀撰夀字伯仁明史方技傳稱為許州人寄居鄞縣案朱右攖寧生傳曰世為許州襄城大家元初祖父官江南自許徙儀真而夀生焉又曰在淮南曰滑夀在呉曰伯仁氏在鄞越曰攖寧生然則許乃祖貫鄞乃寄居實則儀真人也夀卒於明洪武中故明史列之方技傳然戴良九靈山房集有懐滑攖寧詩曰海日蒼涼兩鬢絲異鄉飄泊已多時欲為散木居官道故託長桑説上池蜀客著書人豈識韓公賣藥世偏知道塗同是傷心者只合相從賦黍離則夀亦抱節之遺老託于醫以自逃耳是書首有張翥序稱夀家去東垣近早傳李杲之學攖寧生傳則稱學醫于京口王居中學鍼法于東平髙洞陽考李杲足迹未至江南與夀時代亦不相及翥所云云殆因許近東明附會其説歟難經八十一篇漢藝文志不載隋唐志始載難經二卷秦越人著呉太醫令吕廣嘗註之則其文當出三國前廣書今不傳未審即此本否然唐張守節著史記扁鵲列傳所引難經悉與今合則今書猶古本矣其曰難經者謂經文有疑各設問難以明之其中有此稱經云而素問靈樞無之者則今本素問傳冩有脱今本靈樞乃王冰依託而作非其舊也其文辨析精㣲詞致簡逺讀者不能遽曉故歴代醫家多有註釋夀所採摭凡十一家今惟夀書傳於世其書首列彚考一篇論書之名義源流次列闕誤總類一篇記脱文誤字又次圖説一篇皆不入巻數至其註則融會諸家之説而以巳意折衷之辨論精核考證亦極詳審攖寧生傳稱難經本靈樞素問之㫖設難釋義其間榮衛部位藏府脉法與夫經絡腧穴辨之博矣而闕誤或多愚將本其指義註而讀之即此本也夀本儒者能通解古書文義故其所註視他家所得為多云乾隆四十六年十月恭校上

臣等谨按：《难经本义》二卷，元代滑寿撰。滑寿字伯仁。《明史·方技传》称他是许州人，寄居鄞县。按朱右《撄宁生传》说：滑氏世代是许州襄城的名门望族，元初祖父在江南做官，从许州迁居仪真，滑寿就出生在那里；又说他在淮南称滑寿，在吴地称伯仁氏，在鄞越称撄宁生。这样看来，许州是他的祖籍，鄞县是他的寄居地，实际上他是仪真人。滑寿死于明洪武年间，所以《明史》把他列入《方技传》。然而戴良《九灵山房集》中有《怀滑撄宁》诗说：海日苍凉两鬓丝，异乡飘泊已多时。欲为散木居官道，故托长桑说上池。蜀客著书人岂识，韩公卖药世偏知。道路同是伤心者，只合相从赋黍离。可见滑寿也是固守气节的元代遗民，借行医来自我隐遁罢了。此书开头有张翥的序，称滑寿家离李东垣近，很早就传习李杲的医学；《撄宁生传》则称他向京口王居中学习医术，向东平高洞阳学习针法。考证李杲的足迹未到过江南，与滑寿的时代也不相及，张翥所说的，大概是因为许州靠近东明（李杲籍贯）而附会的说法吧。《难经》八十一篇，《汉书·艺文志》没有记载，《隋书·经籍志》《唐书·艺文志》才开始记载《难经》二卷，说是秦越人所著，吴国太医令吕广曾经作注，那么它的成文应当在三国以前。吕广的注本如今已失传，不知是否就是现在这个本子；但唐代张守节撰写《史记·扁鹊列传正义》所引用的《难经》，全都与今本相合，所以今本还是古本。它之所以叫《难经》，是因为经文有疑难，各自设立问答来加以阐明。其中有标明“经云”而今本《素问》《灵枢》却没有的内容，则是因为今本《素问》传抄有脱漏，而今本《灵枢》是王冰依托伪作，不是它原来的面貌。它的文字辨析精微，辞意简练深远，读者不能很快领悟，所以历代医家多有注释。滑寿所采录的注家共有十一家，如今只有滑寿的书流传于世。此书卷首列《汇考》一篇，论述书名的含义与源流；次列《阙误总类》一篇，记载脱文错字；又次列《图说》一篇，都不计入卷数。至于他的注文，则融汇各家说法而以自己的见解折衷裁定，辨析精核，考证也极为详审。《撄宁生传》称《难经》本着《灵枢》《素问》的旨意设难释义，其中对营卫、部位、脏腑、脉法以及经络、腧穴辨析得很广博，但脱漏错误也很多，我打算本着它的旨意作注来读它，这就是此本。滑寿本是儒生，能够通晓古书文义，所以他的注释比其他各家所得要多。乾隆四十六年十月恭敬校呈。

We, your ministers, respectfully note: The Benyi (Original Meaning) of the Classic of Difficult Issues in two fascicles was written by Hua Shou of the Yuan dynasty. Hua Shou's courtesy name was Boren. The History of Ming (Ming Shi), Biographies of Fangji (Arts and Crafts/Techniques), calls him a native of Xuzhou residing temporarily in Yin County. According to Zhu Right's 'Biography of Yingningsheng,' his family had long been a prominent clan in Xiangcheng, Xuzhou; in the early Yuan, his grandfather held office south of the Yangtze and moved from Xuzhou to Yizhen, where Hua Shou was born. It also states that in Huainan he was called Hua Shou, in Wu he was called Master Boren, and in Yinyue he was called Yingningsheng. Thus Xuzhou was his ancestral home and Yin County his temporary residence, while in fact he was a native of Yizhen. Hua Shou died during the Hongwu reign of the Ming dynasty, which is why the History of Ming placed him in the Fangji biographies. However, Dai Liang's Jiulingshanfang Collection contains a poem 'Missing Hua Yingning,' which says: 'By the desolate sea sun, temples turn gray; wandering in a foreign land for so long. Wishing to be a useless tree along the official road, he therefore borrows Changsang's tale of the upper pool. Who recognizes the Sichuan scholar's writings, while the world especially knows Master Han selling medicine? Travelers on the road are alike grieved at heart, fitting only to chant Shuli together.' Thus Hua Shou was also a loyalist of integrity from the former dynasty who took refuge in medicine to shelter himself. At the beginning of this book is a preface by Zhang Zhu stating that Hua Shou's home was close to Dongyuan and he early inherited the teachings of Li Gao; the 'Biography of Yingningsheng,' however, states that he studied medicine under Wang Juzhong in Jingkou and needling methods under Gao Dongyang in Dongping. Examining the facts, Li Gao never set foot south of the Yangtze and was not contemporary with Hua Shou; what Zhang Zhu said was probably an association made because Xuzhou was near Dongming. The eighty-one sections of the Classic of Difficult Issues are not recorded in the Treatise on Literature of the Book of Han, and only first appear in the treatises of the Book of Sui and the Old/New Books of Tang as Classic of Difficult Issues in two fascicles, attributed to Qin Yueren, with Wu Imperial Physician-in-Chief Lü Guang having annotated it; thus its text must date before the Three Kingdoms. Lü Guang's commentary is now lost, and it is uncertain whether it is this very edition. Yet when Zhang Shoujie of the Tang wrote the Shiji Zhengyi (Correct Meaning of the Records of the Grand Historian) for the biography of Bian Que, all the passages he quoted from the Classic of Difficult Issues match the current text, so the current book is still an ancient text. It is called 'Classic of Difficult Issues' because where the classic text had doubts, it set up questions and difficulties to clarify them. Among them, passages saying 'The Classic states' that are absent from the Suwen and Lingshu are due to omissions in copying the current Suwen, while the current Lingshu was fabricated by Wang Bing under an ancient attribution and is not the original. Its text is subtle in analysis and concise and profound in wording, so readers cannot grasp it at once; hence medical masters across dynasties wrote many commentaries. Hua Shou collected eleven commentators, and today only Hua's book survives. At the front of the book is an 'Examining Compilation' (Huikao) discussing the meaning and origin of the title; next is a 'General Category of Omissions and Errors' recording missing characters and typos; next is 'Illustrated Explanations' (Tushuo), none counted in the fascicle tally. As for his annotations, he synthesizes various scholars' views and adjudicates them with his own judgment, with thorough analysis and detailed textual verification. The 'Biography of Yingningsheng' states that the Classic of Difficult Issues bases itself on the intent of the Lingshu and Suwen to pose difficulties and explain meanings, analyzing nutritive and defensive qi (ying and wei), positions, viscera and bowels (zangfu), pulse methods, as well as channels and acupoints extensively, though with many omissions and errors, and that he would annotate and read it according to its intended meaning—this is that very book. Hua Shou was originally a Confucian scholar able to comprehend ancient texts' meanings, so his annotations yield more than those of other commentators. Respectfully collated and presented in the tenth month of the forty-sixth year of Qianlong.

難經本義序 Preface to Benyi of the Classic of Difficult Issues (by Zhang Zhu).

醫之為道聖矣自神農氏凡草木金石可濟夫夭死札瘥悉列諸經而八十一難自秦越人推本軒岐鬼3099區之書發難析疑論辯精詣鬼神無遁情為萬世法其道與天地並立功豈小補也哉且夫以人七尺之軀五藏百骸受病六氣之沴乃繫於三指㸃按之下一呼一吸之間無有形影特切其洪細濡伏若一2212苟或謬誤則脉生而藥死之矣而可輕以談醫而可易以習醫邪寓鄞滑伯仁故家許許去東垣近蚤為李氏之學遂名于醫予雅聞之未識也今年秋來遺所譔難經本義閲之使人起敬有是哉君之精意於醫也條釋圖陳脉絡尺寸部1974虚實蕳而通决而明予雖未嘗學而思亦過半矣嗚呼醫之道生道也道行則生意充宇宙澤流無窮人以夀死是則徃聖之心也世之學者能各置一通於側而深求力討之不為良醫也者㡬希嗚呼越人我師也伯仁不為我而刋諸梓與天下之人共之是則伯仁之心也故舉其大指為序至正二十五年龍集甲辰十月旣望翰林學士承㫖榮禄大夫知制誥國史張翥序

医学之道崇高神圣啊！自从神农氏以来，凡是草木金石等可以用来救治夭折、瘟疫的药物，都载入各种药书；而《八十一难》，自秦越人推求黄帝、岐伯、鬼臾区等古医经的本源，提出疑难、剖析疑问，论辩精深透彻，连鬼神也藏不住实情，成为万世的准则，它的道理与天地并立，功劳岂止是微小的补益呢！再说人有七尺之躯、五脏百骸，感受外邪六气之厉气，病情竟系于三根手指点按之下；在短短一呼一吸之间，没有可见的形影，单凭切按脉象的洪、细、濡、伏等变化，如果稍有差错，就会出现“脉主生而用药致死”的后果，怎么可以轻率地谈论医学，怎么可以轻易地学习医学呢？寄居鄞县的滑伯仁，旧家在许州，许州离李东垣很近，他很早就研习李氏的医学，因而以医术闻名。我素来听闻他的名声却未曾相见；今年秋天他来访，留赠所撰《难经本义》，我阅读之后令人肃然起敬。真是这样啊，您对医学用心如此精专！分条阐释、绘图列明经络、寸口尺部的脉位与虚实，简明而通达、果决而明晰。我虽然没有学过医，但领悟也超过一半了。唉，医学之道是生生之道啊！医道行于世，生机就充满天地，恩泽流传无穷，使人都能享尽天年，这就是古代圣贤的心愿啊。世上的学医之人如果能够各自在案旁放一部，深入探求、努力钻研，不成良医的人恐怕很少了。唉，秦越人是我的老师；滑伯仁不只是为了我，而是将书刊刻流传，与天下人共享，这就是滑伯仁的心愿啊。所以概括它的主旨作序。至正二十五年（1365年），岁次甲辰，十月既望（十六日），翰林学士承旨、荣禄大夫、知制诰、修国史张翥作序。

The way of medicine is sublime and sacred! Since Shennong, all herbs, woods, metals, and stones that can rescue people from premature death and epidemics have been set forth in various classics; as for the Eighty-One Difficult Issues, from Qin Yueren tracing the root of the writings of Xuanyuan (the Yellow Emperor), Qi Bo, and Guiyuqu to raise difficulties and resolve doubts, his arguments are penetrating and thorough, so that even ghosts and spirits could not hide their true state, serving as a standard for ten thousand generations—its way stands alongside Heaven and Earth, and its merit is no small supplement! Moreover, in a human body of seven chi with five viscera and a hundred bones, contracting noxious six-qi pathogens depends upon the pressing of three fingers; within the span of a single exhale and inhale, with no visible form or shadow, one discerns surging, fine, soggy, or hidden pulses. If there is any error, the pulse may indicate life while the medication kills the patient—how then can one talk of medicine lightly, or learn medicine easily? Hua Boren, residing in Yin County, had an ancestral home in Xuzhou; because Xuzhou is close to Li Dongyuan, he early studied Master Li's teachings and became famous in medicine. I had long heard of him without meeting; this autumn he came and left his drafted Benyi of the Classic of Difficult Issues. Reading it inspires respect—indeed, how refined and devoted is your mind to medicine! Section by section explained and illustrated, laying out channel pathways, cun, chi, and pulse locations, and vacuity and repletion, concise yet clear, decisive yet distinct. Though I have never studied medicine, my understanding already exceeds half. Alas, the way of medicine is the way of life! When the way is practiced, life-giving vitality fills the cosmos and beneficence flows endlessly, allowing people to die of natural longevity—this is the heart of the ancient sages. If students of medicine in the world each keep a copy at hand and deeply investigate and strive with it, few will fail to become good physicians. Alas, Yueren is my teacher; Boren did not write it merely for me, but engraved it on woodblocks to share with all under heaven—this is Boren's heart. Therefore I summarize its main purport as a preface. In the twenty-fifth year of Zhizheng, cyclical year jiachen, the sixteenth day of the tenth month (jiwang), preface by Zhang Zhu, Academician Recipient of Edicts of the Hanlin Academy, Grand Master for Splendid Happiness, Drafter of Proclamations, and Compiler of National History.

Benyi of the Classic of Difficult Issues is an exposition written by Hua Shou of Xuchang based on the meaning of the Classic of Difficult Issues. The Classic of Difficult Issues is traditionally said to have been authored by Qin Yueren of Bohai, yet the Shiji does not record it, and only the bibliographic treatises of the Books of Sui and Tang list the title Classic of Eighty-One Difficult Issues of the Yellow Emperor by Qin Yueren in two fascicles. Could it be that disciples transmitted it privately among themselves at that time and the Grand Historian happened not to see it? Examining the Shiji Zhengyi and various scholars' accounts, that it is Yueren's work is beyond doubt. Roughly speaking, it takes the intent of the Yellow Emperor's Suwen and Lingshu as its basis and sets up questions and answers to resolve doubtful meanings; therein, nutritive and defensive qi (ying and wei), measurements and degrees (dushu), cun and chi positions, yin and yang flourishing and declining, internal and external viscera and bowels, pulse methods, disease manifestations (bing tai), as well as channel circulation, needling, and acupoints are all completely included. Its words are concise and its meanings broad, serving to expand the ancients and enlighten later generations out of deep solicitude for the people. Down through the ages, commentators have followed one another to perhaps several score, yet some err in prolixity and some in excessive brevity, pure and impure are mixed together, and right and wrong attack each other; moreover, since the book went through the fire when Hua Tuo burned his manuscripts in prison, missing characters and displaced slips inevitably leave regrets. In all matters of the world, following their origin establishes their principle, and dredging their source lengthens their flow; there is no case of basing oneself on a text's meaning without grasping its intent. Just as the ancient Book of Changes was originally set up for divination, and Master Zhu (Zhu Xi) traced back to images and prognostications to compose Benyi so that the minds of the four sages became clear, Benyi of the Classic of Difficult Issues modestly borrows from this method. Therefore, I examine the Lingshu and Suwen above to probe its source, connect with Zhang Zhongjing and Wang Shuhe below to trace its thread, broadly gather and verify whatever is good among various views, and seek out missing text and broken slips carefully, while following ancient scholars' convention of transmitting doubts where uncertain. Alas, times have earlier and later, yet principles have no ancient or modern; grasping its meaning yields its principle, and grasping its principle means the author's mind across a hundred generations does not go beyond it. Even so, in this exposition I dare not claim it has already reached perfection; if later gentlemen see where it falls short and revise and correct it, is that not fitting? The Biography of Bian Que in the Shiji records his treatment of three illnesses—Zhao Jianzi, the Crown Prince of Guo, and Duke/Marquis Huan of Qi (with garbled characters in the source)—but has no statement that he authored the Classic of Difficult Issues. Both the Treatise on Literature in the Book of Sui and the Treatise on Literature in the Book of Tang list the title Classic of Eighty-One Difficult Issues of the Yellow Emperor by Qin Yueren in two fascicles; moreover, Zhang Shoujie, Reader-in-Waiting to the Imperial Princes of the Tang, wrote the Shiji Zhengyi, quoting passages of the Classic of Difficult Issues in full in the biography of Bian Que and Cang Gong to explain their meaning, and after the biography recording in full the Forty-Second Difficult Issue as well as the First and Thirty-Seventh Difficult Issues. From this we know that ancient accounts attributing it to Yueren are not false. Examining closely the wording of its questions, passages saying 'The Classic states' derive from texts of the Suwen and Lingshu, especially in the Lingshu; there are also passages not found in either classic—could Yueren have drawn from another ancient classic, or set up questions and answers himself? Master Yu of Shao'an (Yu Ji) once said: the Shiji does not record Yueren authoring the Classic of Difficult Issues, whereas the bibliographic treatises of the Sui and Tang histories formally enter Yueren's Classic of Difficult Issues, and the author of the Shiji Zhengyi directly records several chapters of the Classic of Difficult Issues. My own view is that the ancients raised difficulties based on classics, or perhaps in question-and-answer with disciples happened to produce these eighty-one chapters, not necessarily that there were only eighty-one points in the classic worth questioning; the questions arose from the classic itself rather than being set forth as an independent treatise (the source's 'wei' is a slip for 'jing,' classic), and moreover the ancients did not seek to attach their names to books, so transmission was confined to specialized masters. Later, as circulation gradually widened, government offices could copy and catalog it, and commentators could quote it and weave it into their writings.

💡 本段考据《难经》成书与流传：（1）《史记》载扁鹊医案：诊赵简子昏迷、虢太子“尸厥”、齐桓侯（《韩非子·喻老》《史记》作齐桓侯）腠理至骨髓之病，体现了“病在腠理，汤熨之所及也”的早期疾病分期与治则思想。（2）《难经》一难论独取寸口脉法，三十七难论五脏之气上通九窍与关格，四十二难论肠胃长短、容量与五脏重量。针灸与解剖生理学习中，这些篇章是了解古人脉学与脏腑度量的重要文献。

Master Ouyang of Guizhai (Ouyang Xuan) said: taking the pulse at the cun kou (radial artery at the wrist) of the hand began with Qin Yueren, so he is likely the progenitor of physicians. The Classic of Difficult Issues is an ancient pre-Qin text, and Han-dynasty and later works like 'Replying to a Guest's Difficulties' came after it; it is also the progenitor of literary works that debate and question one another.

💡 元代欧阳玄之言。需注意学术史辨析：清代及现代学者多认为《难经》成书于汉代（具体年代说法不一），并非先秦古文；“寸口诊法自秦越人始”也需结合《内经》“气口”概念综合看待，但《难经·一难》明确提出“十二经皆有动脉，独取寸口，以决五脏六腑死生吉凶之法”，奠定了后世寸口诊脉的核心地位。寸口位于桡动脉搏动处，临近手太阴肺经经穴太渊（LU9）、经渠（LU8）、列缺（LU7）。

杨玄操序说：黄帝有《内经》二帙（即《素问》与《灵枢》），它的义理幽深隐晦，几乎难以穷尽阅览；秦越人于是摘取两部经书中的精要共八十一章，推演阐发其中的道理，命名为《八十一难经》，因为它的理趣深远、不是仓促之间容易明白的缘故。

Yang Xuancao's preface states that the Yellow Emperor had two sets of the Neijing (Suwen and Lingshu), whose meanings are obscure and profound and almost impossible to fathom completely; Yueren therefore selected essential points from both classics totaling eighty-one chapters and expanded their principles, naming it the Classic of Eighty-One Difficult Issues because its rationale is deep and far-reaching and not easily grasped at a glance.

💡 点明《难经》与《内经》的提炼与推演关系。对针灸学习而言，《难经》对《内经》中的藏象、脉法、经络、五输穴、原穴、八会穴等作了进一步归纳与系统化。

北宋治平年间（1064—1067年），京兆黎秦辰为虞庶《难经注》作序说：世间流传《黄帝八十一难经》，之所以称为“难”，莫非是因为人的五脏六腑隐蔽在体内，被邪气侵犯后难以测度知晓，只能凭借脉理来推究它的仿佛吗？比如有的脉象重如十二颗豆子（菽），又有像按着车盖、像抚摸鸡羽一样的脉象，再结合内外证候来参详印证，难道不难吗？按欧阳玄、虞集的说法，“难”字应当读去声（nàn，发难、问难）；其余各家都读平声（奴丹切，nán，困难）。

During the Zhiping era of the Song (1064–1067), Li Qinchen of Jingzhao wrote in his preface to Yu Shu's Commentary on the Classic of Difficult Issues: Regarding the widely transmitted Classic of Eighty-One Difficult Issues of the Yellow Emperor, is it called 'difficult' (nan/nàn) because the five viscera and six bowels are hidden within the body and, when invaded by pathogens, cannot be measured or known directly, so one can only investigate their semblance through pulse principles? For instance, some pulses weigh like twelve beans (shu), while others feel like pressing a carriage canopy or stroking chicken feathers, and one must also examine internal and external signs to cross-check them—is that not difficult? According to Ouyang Xuan and Yu Ji, the title word should be read in the departing tone (nàn, 'to pose questions'); all the other authors read it in the level tone (nán, 'difficult').

Ding Deyong's Supplementary Commentary notes that the Classic of Difficult Issues was transmitted across generations to only one person at a time, until in the Wei dynasty Hua Tuo burned his manuscripts in prison; between the Jin and Song, although there were the works of Zhang Zhongjing and Wang Shuhe, each set forth its own text and initiated its theories, and when Lü Guang, Imperial Physician-in-Chief of Wu, recompiled this classic, its wording and meaning still had misplaced sequences. 火是木的‘子’。‘子能令母实’，是说子气有余，就不去取食于母（母因此也有余）；如今泻南方火，是夺去子气，使它转而取食于母（母实也就随之而减）。金是水的‘母’。‘母能令子虚’，是说母气不足，就不能荫养其子；如今补北方水，是增益子气，子就不至于再取食于母。这与《第八十一难》的义理正可互相发明；其中所说‘不能治其虚，安问其余’，就隐含着不可‘实实虚虚’的意思。

Fire is the 'child' of wood. 'The child can make the mother excessive' means that when the child has a surplus it does not feed on its mother (so the mother too is in surplus); now, draining the south (fire) takes away the child's qi and makes it feed on its mother, reducing her. Metal is the 'mother' of water. 'The mother can make the child deficient' means that when the mother is insufficient she cannot shelter her child; now, supplementing the north (water) increases the child's qi so that it no longer needs to feed on its mother. This and the Eighty-first Difficult Issue illuminate each other; the statement 'If one cannot treat the deficiency, why ask about the rest?' quietly carries the warning against 'making the full fuller and the empty emptier.'

💡 【术语】“子能令母实、母能令子虚”与“泻南方、补北方”出自《难经·七十五难》：东方（木、肝）实，西方（金、肺）虚，则泻南方（火、心）、补北方（水、肾）。“实实虚虚”指误治：使实的更实、虚的更虚（见八十一难）。【医理】火为木之子，泻火即夺木之气；水为金之子，补水则水不再耗金，肺金得以恢复，再去制约肝木。【古今】五行母子是古人推演补泻的理论模型，没有对应的解剖结构；针灸教学中仍以此讲解“泻南补北”与子母补泻配穴。【考试】七十五难“泻南补北”、六十九难“虚者补其母，实者泻其子”常对照出题。
